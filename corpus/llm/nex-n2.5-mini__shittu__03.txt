Language is not a decoration placed on culture after the fact; it is one of the ways culture learns to think, to remember, to punish, to bless, and to imagine itself. In Nigeria, where Igbo, Yoruba, Hausa, Kanuri, Efik, Ijaw, Tiv, and English often meet in the same street, the same family, the same sermon, the same song, language becomes a small theatre of belonging. To speak one tongue at home and another in the schoolroom is to live inside a divided but fruitful grammar, where identity is not fixed like a passport photograph but negotiated like a proverb that must be finished before the listener understands the joke. The mother’s word carries the heat of the kitchen, the market, the ancestral shrine, the church pew, the mosque courtyard; the schoolword carries examination paper, official stamp, railway timetable, and the cold arithmetic of empire. Yet neither should be despised. The inherited language keeps memory from becoming abstract, while the borrowed or imposed language, when reclaimed, can become a bridge rather than a wound.

This is why the question of representation matters. A people’s story is never told only by archives, maps, or state documents; it is also told by the sounds children swallow before sleeping, by lullabies that fold history into rhythm, by idioms that cannot be translated without losing the sweat in their bones.
