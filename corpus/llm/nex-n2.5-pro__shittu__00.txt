The relationship between memory and landscape in contemporary Nigerian writing is not merely decorative; it is an argument about belonging, loss, and historical representation. The land is not a passive background on which human sorrow is displayed. Rather, it becomes a living archive, a discourse through which the community remembers what official history often forgets. In this sense, the river, the market road, the baobab tree, and the dust of the harmattan function like witnesses. They hold the traces of migration, colonial violence, civil war, and ordinary labour. Like Achebe’s red earth, the landscape carries both fertility and danger; like Soyinka’s market, it is a space where the living and the ancestral meet; and, unlike Wordsworth’s serene lakes, it is rarely innocent or detached from political pain. The poet therefore turns geography into narrative, making place speak in a language older than the archive yet more immediate than the textbook.

We buried our names beneath the iroko,  
and the rain kept our syllables green.  
When the road forgot the village
