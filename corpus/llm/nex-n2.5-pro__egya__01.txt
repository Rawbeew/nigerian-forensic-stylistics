When the village square puts on Wi-Fi,

the iroko keeps its old silence,

though children now measure moonlight

by the brightness of borrowed screens,

and grandmothers, who once read the smoke

of cooking fires better than pastors read scripture,

stand at the threshold of cement houses

watching their grandchildren salute ghosts

with the wrong fingers.

We have eaten the world,

yes,

and the world has eaten us back

with plastic spoons,

with songs that arrive before rain,

with English fattened in the mouth

like a government cheque,

while the mother tongue,

that thin hen scratching near the kitchen wall,

keeps laying small bright eggs

no market woman wants to buy.

What will the ancestors say,

ndi ichie,

when the ogene is drowned

by a ringtone from nowhere,

when the masquerade returns from the bush

and finds its name trending on a phone

held by a boy who cannot speak to fire?

The river Niger, old carrier of wounds,

still bends northward and southward,

still remembers
