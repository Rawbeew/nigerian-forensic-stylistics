When the harmattan lowers its gray mouth over the red earth,

silence gathers in the cassava leaves,

in the cracked calabash beside the well,

in the railway sleepers where colonial names

once slept like iron snakes,

and I, who was born after the guns had rusted

but not after their echoes,

know that what is left unsaid

does not die;

it burrows under the compound,

it waits in the yam barn,

it rises at dusk with the mosquitoes

and enters the room where the old women

pretend to remember only the price of pepper.

Ndi mmuo, stand close,

because the children are asking why the mango tree

bends toward the road as if listening for footsteps

that never returned from Aba, from Umuahia,

from the forest where hunger wore a soldier’s belt.

The river Niger carries everything:

bottles, prayers, the shadow of bridges,
