The boundary between the natural and the political is not a fence but a negotiation, a weathered line drawn through river, forest, oil, bone, and memory. In Nigeria, where the Niger moves with an intelligence older than the map, nature is not a passive backdrop to history; it is a participant in discourse, a silent archivist of invasion, harvest, flood, and extraction. The delta, for instance, does not simply “contain” oil; it bears the political meaning of oil, the promise and the violence attached to it, the way foreign appetite enters the body of the land and calls itself progress. Like Ovid’s metamorphosis, transformation here is not always beautiful. It is the river changing color, the mangrove choking on machinery, the fisherman reading the tide as both omen and indictment.
