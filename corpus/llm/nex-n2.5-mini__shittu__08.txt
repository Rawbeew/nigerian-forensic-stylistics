The silence in a room after accusation is not empty; it is crowded with the furniture of fear, with chairs pulled close, with hands folded as if prayer and defense could be mistaken for one another. To speak is to enter the discourse, to risk the sentence that fixes the unsaid into something others may repeat, distort, punish. A child learns this before grammar: the pause before the slap, the lowered voice that means danger, the mother’s cough that asks the father to stop. Later, one calls it trauma, or history, or the politics of the body, but the first knowledge is domestic, intimate, carried under the tongue like a stone. In the old stories, Oedipus blinds himself because sight has become unbearable; in our own country, Okonkwo kills and is killed by a world that has already changed its grammar. Between Sophocles and Umuofia there is a common chamber, and in it silence is not innocence. It is a narrative instrument, a witness that has learned to wear the mask of obedience.

Yet silence is not always the language of the guilty. Sometimes it is the only shelter left to the violated, the last room in a house where every wall has become testimony. The woman who does not speak has not necessarily consented; the elder who lowers his eyes may be measuring the cost of truth; the soldier who remembers nothing may remember too much. To demand speech is to risk making suffering into evidence, to turn pain into a document for the convenience of those who prefer neat columns to trembling lives. Here the Western and the Nigerian meet: Kafka’s court waits outside every door, while Soyinka’s masquerade moves through the market with a face that is also a revelation. The unsaid can be a refusal of representation, a protest against being converted into symbol, case study, lesson.
