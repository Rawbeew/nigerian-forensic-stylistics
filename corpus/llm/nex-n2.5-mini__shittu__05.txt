To speak of movement is to enter a discourse already crowded with banners, borders, and borrowed griefs. The migrant is often represented as a figure without history, as if the suitcase were a complete biography. Yet in the Nigerian imagination, from the market roads of Onitsha to the crowded ferries of Lagos, movement has never been merely flight; it is also method, appetite, negotiation. Odysseus may carry his longing across the wine-dark sea, and Aeneas may lift his father from burning Troy, but the Nigerian traveller knows that home can travel in a tin of pepper soup, in a proverb spoken too quickly, in the precise shade of red soil remembered from a village not always willingly left.

The sense of home, then, is not a fixed coordinate but a narrative arrangement. It is made and unmade by departure, by the shame of accent, by the new grammar of queues, visas, and night buses. Wole Soyinka’s dark courtroom of exile and Achebe’s broken towns remind us that displacement is never only private; it belongs to history, to empire, to the violent rearrangement of peoples.
