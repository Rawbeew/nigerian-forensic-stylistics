At the turning from harmattan to rain, the village does not simply begin again; it rehearses its beginning. The women sweep the compounds before the first thunder, clearing not only dust but the old arguments that have settled there in the dry months: debts, names of the dead, the stubbornness of those who left and did not return. The children collect fallen twigs, while the elders speak carefully, as if language itself might frighten the season away. Here, ritual is not decoration but discipline, a way of teaching the body to move with what it cannot master. The harmattan has made everything brittle: skin, memory, the red earth cracked like old pottery. Yet the season also reveals what has survived its heat. In this sense, loss is not only absence; it is the exposed shape of what remains.

The market at dusk becomes a small theatre of return. Smokers lay fish beside bowls of palm wine; traders fold their cloth with the precision of people who know that visibility is a form of power. Someone sings an old tune whose verses change every year, and in the change we hear both Orpheus descending and the griot carrying names through fire. We imagine the dead not as gone but as unassigned, waiting for the right season, the right voice, the right amount of water poured at the root of the bitter leaf. To mark the season is to admit that time is not a line but a house with many doors: the door of planting, the door of mourning, the door through which a mother passes after losing a child, the door through which the rain passes after a long absence.

In the language of discourse, the rains return the world to narrative. What the dry months fractured into silence becomes again story, representation, shared breath. The farmer opens the furrow; the widow lights a small lamp; the boy names the first swallow, though it is only a swallow in another country’s poem. We inherit many ghosts: Antigone at the gate, Odysse
